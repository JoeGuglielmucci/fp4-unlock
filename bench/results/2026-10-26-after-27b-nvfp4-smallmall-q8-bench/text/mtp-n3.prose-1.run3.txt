"Speculative decoding speeds up large language model inference by using a small, fast draft model to propose several future tokens before the large target model is run. The draft model generates a short candidate sequence, often greedily or with a simple sampling policy, in a few cheap forward passes. The target model then processes the original prompt plus the proposed tokens in one forward pass, computing logits for every position in that sequence. This lets it verify multiple draft tokens at once instead of generating them one by one.

The key trick is rejection sampling. For each proposed token, the target model compares its own probability for that token with the draft model’s probability. If the target model’s probability is at least as high, the token is accepted. If it is lower, the token is rejected and a new token is sampled from a corrected distribution that favors the target model’s probabilities while preserving the target model’s exact output distribution. After the first rejected token, the remaining draft tokens are discarded. Because every accepted or resampled token is drawn from the target model’s conditional distribution, the final sequence has the same distribution as ordinary autoregressive decoding with the target model alone.

In practice, speedup depends on how often the draft model’s proposals are accepted, the cost of running the target model on the longer sequence, and the relative latency of the draft model. A high acceptance rate and a cheap draft model produce large gains, while a slow draft model or expensive verification reduces them. The two most common reasons the speedup is smaller than hoped are low draft-target agreement, especially on difficult or diverse prompts, and the overhead of running the draft model plus the target model’s longer verification pass."
