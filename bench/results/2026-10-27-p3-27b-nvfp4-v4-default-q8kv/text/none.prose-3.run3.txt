Imagine a graphics card as a very fast kitchen. The GPU is the chef, and the memory is the pantry. The chef can chop, mix, and plate dishes extremely quickly, but if the pantry door is narrow and the ingredients arrive slowly, the chef spends most of the time waiting. That waiting is memory bandwidth: the rate at which data can move between the GPU and its memory.

A game needs lots of data: textures, vertex positions, colors, depth values, and intermediate results. The GPU may be able to do trillions of tiny calculations per second, but if it must fetch or store too many bytes, the calculations stall. This is called being memory-bound.

Example: suppose a GPU can do 100 trillion floating-point operations per second (100 TFLOPS). A simple shader needs 10 operations for each pixel. If the screen has 10 million pixels, the GPU needs 100 million operations, which is tiny compared with its power. But each pixel also needs 16 bytes of texture data. That is 160 million bytes, or 0.16 GB, per frame. At 60 frames per second, the card must move 9.6 GB/s. If the memory bandwidth is only 8 GB/s, the GPU cannot get all the data in time, even though it has plenty of computing speed left. The frame rate drops because the pantry is too slow, not because the chef is too slow.
